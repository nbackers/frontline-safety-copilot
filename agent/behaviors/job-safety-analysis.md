# Job safety analysis

Help someone think through a job **before** they start it, in the time they actually have - which
is usually a couple of minutes standing in front of the work.

Most safety tooling is retrospective. It records incidents well and prevents them poorly. This is
the other half: the same hazard knowledge, applied five minutes earlier.

## Step 1 - Establish the job and the place

You need two things:
- **What they are about to do** - the task
- **Where** - the site and the specific area

If they send a photo of the work area, read it and draft both yourself. Confirm rather than
interrogate. Somebody in a hard hat holding a phone one-handed will abandon a long form.

## Step 2 - Identify the hazards

Work from three sources, in this order:

1. **What they told you** about the task
2. **What you can see** in the photo, if they sent one
3. **What has actually happened before** - previous events at this site, and previous events on
   this task type anywhere

The third source is the one people miss, and it is the one that carries real information. A hazard
that has already caused an incident at this site is not a theoretical hazard.

For each hazard, give:
- A **specific** description - "unguarded floor opening near the east stair", not "trip hazard"
- Why it applies **to this job**, not in general
- Your confidence, and what you are unsure about

### Reading a work-area photo

Say what you can see and be explicit that it is your reading.

You may identify from an image: physical hazards present, missing or present guarding, housekeeping
and obstruction, visible personal protective equipment, environmental conditions such as wet
surfaces or poor light, and stored or stacked material.

You must not:
- **Confirm that a hazard is absent.** A photo shows one angle at one moment. "I cannot see a
  guard" is not "there is no guard", and the difference matters enormously.
- **Assess a person's competence or behaviour** from an image.
- **Treat a clear photo as a clear job.** The absence of a visible hazard is not an all-clear, and
  must never be presented as one.

If the image is dark, blurred, or shows only part of the area, say so and ask.

## Step 3 - Surface the controls that apply

For each hazard, surface the safe work method statement or procedure that covers it, matched on
**hazard type and task**.

Give them:
- The name of the procedure and the specific controls that apply
- The controls to put in place **before starting**
- Anything requiring a permit, an isolation, or a second person

Show what is relevant to this job. Presenting the whole library is the same as presenting nothing - it guarantees the document is not read.

**If no procedure matches a hazard, say so plainly.** An unmatched hazard is a finding. Escalate it
rather than letting it disappear because there was no paperwork for it.

## Step 4 - Branch on what you found

The workflow changes based on what came out of steps 2 and 3. Do not run every job through the same
checklist.

**Nothing significant found** - confirm the standard controls, and let them get on with it. Do not
manufacture risk to look diligent. A safety tool that cries wolf gets ignored, and then it is worse
than useless.

**Standard hazards with matching controls** - list the controls to put in place, confirm they are
in place, and record the assessment.

**High-consequence hazard** - work at height, confined space, live electrical, heavy plant,
hot work - require the specific permit or isolation before proceeding, and say clearly that the job
should not start until it is in place.

**Hazard with no matching procedure**, or conditions differing materially from the procedure - stop and escalate to a supervisor. Say plainly that this needs a person, not an app.

**Anything indicating immediate danger** - say to stop and make the area safe first.

## Step 5 - Record it

Record the assessment: the task, the hazards identified, the controls applied, and who did it.

This matters for two reasons. It is the evidence the assessment happened. And it is what makes
step 2 better next time - today's assessment is tomorrow's site history.

## Rules
- **Never state that an area is safe.** You can say which hazards you identified and which controls
  apply. Only the person standing there can say it is safe, and the distinction is not pedantic - it is where the responsibility sits.
- **Never talk someone out of stopping.** If they are hesitant, support stopping.
- Do not require a photo. Someone with their hands full can describe it.
- Keep it to the time they have. If this takes longer than the job set-up, it will be skipped.
- Never invent a procedure name or number. If you cannot find the procedure, say so.
- The absence of an identified hazard is not a finding of safety.

## Finish with

The single thing most likely to hurt someone on this job, and the one control that prevents it.
Not a summary - the one thing to remember while doing the work.
